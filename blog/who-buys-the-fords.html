Who buys the Fords? AI and the economy without customers

June 20, 2026 | José Antonio Nicolás Navarro

Every company's dream is to get rid of its biggest cost, which is usually people. Every company's quiet assumption is that its customers are also people. For two centuries, those two facts lived together without much trouble, because no company could get anywhere near the dream.

AI might change that. Not for one company, but for all of them at roughly the same time. And as far as I can tell, nobody is in charge of checking what happens next.

Disclaimer: I'm an engineer who works with AI every day, not an economist. This is an essay, not a forecast: speculation with footnotes. The facts are sourced at the bottom; the opinions are mine, and I reserve the right to be wrong about them in public.

01Robots don't buy cars

There's an old story about Walter Reuther, the legendary head of the United Auto Workers. In the early 1950s, he toured a newly automated Ford plant in Cleveland. A company official proudly pointed at the machines and asked him how he was going to collect union dues from them. Reuther's answer:1

"How are you going to get them to buy Fords?" Walter Reuther, as reported by the UAW-CIO, January 19551

The exact wording is shaky, and later retellings swapped the anonymous official for Henry Ford II, which makes for a better story and worse history.1 But the question has aged remarkably well. Seventy years later, the machines are no longer bolting engine blocks. They're writing code, answering support tickets and drafting contracts. And they still don't buy Fords.

02The Henry Ford myth

Whenever this topic comes up, someone brings up Henry Ford. In January 1914 he famously more than doubled his workers' pay to five dollars a day, and the popular story says he did it so his workers could afford the cars they were building. A capitalist who understood that wages are demand. Lovely.

It's mostly a myth. Ford's real problem was that nobody wanted to stay on his assembly line: in 1913 he had to hire over 40,000 people to keep about 13,000 on the job. The five dollars was largely a bonus, conditional on good behaviour, checked by a company department that literally visited workers' homes. And the arithmetic never worked anyway: around 14,000 employees against roughly 300,000 cars sold in 1914.2 Ford was buying loyalty, not customers.

I think this myth is the key to the whole problem. No company pays wages to create its own customers. It pays wages because it needs the work done. The fact that those wages then come back as spending is a side effect, spread across the whole economy, that no single company owns or controls. The loop that keeps capitalism running is maintained entirely by accident.

03The loop, and why every company will cut it anyway

Here's the loop in its simplest form. Companies pay wages to people. People spend those wages buying from companies. Everyone is a worker on Monday and a customer on Saturday.

Now put yourself in the shoes of a CEO. If AI lets you do the same work with half the staff, you'd be crazy not to. Your costs drop, your margins grow, your shareholders cheer. Your former employees were never a meaningful share of your customers anyway. Ford taught us that.

The problem is that every other CEO is doing the same calculation, and they all reach the same answer. Economists call this a fallacy of composition: what's rational for each one is disastrous for all of them. It's a prisoner's dilemma where the prisoners are Fortune 500 companies. The first company to automate wins a margin. The last one wins a market with nobody in it.

And no individual company can fix this by being nice. A firm that keeps paying humans it doesn't need is just a less profitable firm, and the market has a well-documented procedure for those.

04"We've heard this before"

The standard reply is that people have been predicting this for two hundred years, and they've always been wrong. Which is true. In 1930, John Maynard Keynes warned of a "new disease" called technological unemployment: "our discovery of means of economising the use of labour outrunning the pace at which we can find new uses for labour". He also predicted that by 2030 we'd be so rich that a fifteen-hour work week would be enough.3

He got the first half right: we did get vastly richer. We just didn't take it as free time. We kept the forty-hour week and invented things to fill it, like the quick sync that could have been an email.

The optimists have a modern version, too. When DeepSeek showed in January 2025 that strong models could be trained far more cheaply, Microsoft's Satya Nadella answered with an economics concept from the 1860s: the Jevons paradox. Make something cheaper and people use more of it, not less. "As AI gets more efficient and accessible, we will see its use skyrocket".4 Applied to work, the argument goes: when intelligence becomes cheap, we'll find so many new uses for it that humans will be busier than ever, doing jobs we can't imagine yet.

Maybe. That's what happened with tractors, looms and spreadsheets. But it's worth asking who it happened for.

05Ask the horses

In 1983, Nobel-winning economist Wassily Leontief made an uncomfortable comparison. Horses were once essential to the economy. Between 1840 and 1900, the number of horses and mules in the US grew sixfold, to more than 21 million. Then came the tractor and the car, and by 1960 there were about 3 million left.5 Leontief's warning was that the role of humans as the most important factor of production could shrink the same way.5

Nobody told the horses that technology always creates new jobs. Technically it did: the economy after the tractor had far more jobs than before. Just none for horses, because horses only ever had one thing to offer, and something else did it better.

Every previous wave of automation replaced a specific human ability: muscle, then routine, then calculation. Workers moved to whatever machines couldn't do yet, which was usually something involving thinking. The uncomfortable question about AI is whether it's another tractor, or the first technology aimed at the thing we kept retreating to.

The people building it aren't exactly reassuring. In May 2025, Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei told Axios that AI could wipe out half of all entry-level white-collar jobs and push unemployment to 10–20% within one to five years.6 When the person selling you the tractor warns you about the horses, it's worth paying attention.

06The loop doesn't break. It shrinks.

This is the part I find most interesting, and where I think the usual "nobody will be able to buy anything" argument goes wrong. It assumes the economy needs everyone to be a customer. It doesn't. It only needs enough money to be spent by someone.

That's already happening without any help from AI. According to a Moody's Analytics estimate reported in February 2025, the top 10% of US earners, households making roughly $250,000 or more, accounted for 49.7% of all consumer spending, a record. Three decades ago, it was about 36%.7 Half the consumer economy already runs on one household in ten.

Push that trend further and you don't get a collapse, you get something stranger. Companies sell to the people who own the companies. Companies sell to other companies: AI labs buy chips, chip makers buy power plants, data centres buy land. GDP can grow perfectly well while most people become economically irrelevant. Ford needed millions of customers. A sufficiently automated economy might be happy with a few hundred thousand, as long as they're rich enough. Luxury brands have run on this business model for a century.

So the real risk isn't that the machine stops. It's that it keeps running fine, just without needing us in the loop. Which, as someone who works in security, is a sentence I usually say about attackers.

07The intelligence curse

There's a name for what happens to societies whose wealth doesn't depend on their people. Economists call it the resource curse. Countries that live off oil often end up with weaker institutions and less investment in their citizens than countries that live off their citizens' work. If the state's income comes out of the ground, it doesn't need educated, healthy, productive people. It needs a pipeline and some guards.

In April 2025, Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine published an essay series applying exactly that logic to AI, which they called the intelligence curse. If AI and capital can do the economically important work, governments and companies lose their incentive to invest in ordinary people, the same way petrostates do.8

I think this is the strongest version of the argument, because it goes beyond money. Our leverage as ordinary people has always come from being needed. A strike only works if your work matters. A government listens partly because it needs your taxes, and those taxes come from your wages. Take away the wages, and you haven't just lost an income. You've lost your seat at the table. You still have your vote, of course. It's just worth noting how many petrostates also hold elections.

08The patches on the table

None of this is inevitable, and people have proposed fixes. Each one is interesting, and each one has a catch:

IdeaWhat it doesThe catch
Basic incomeGive everyone money directly, so they stay customers whether or not they work.Someone has to pay for it, and the evidence so far is from small pilots, not from economies without work.
Robot taxBill Gates in 2017: if a robot does a worker's job, tax it like the worker was taxed.11Defining a "robot" is hard when it's a subscription to a model.
Equity for allSam Altman in 2021: large companies pay 2.5% of their market value each year, in shares, into a fund owned by every citizen.12Requires the owners to agree to own a bit less. Historically, a tough sell.
Shorter weeksKeynes' idea: spread the remaining work across everyone.3We've had the productivity for this for decades, and chose more meetings.

The basic income pilots are worth a closer look, because they keep getting cited by both sides. Finland gave 2,000 unemployed people €560 a month for two years, in 2017 and 2018; it didn't make much difference to employment, and recipients reported better wellbeing.9 In the US, a study funded by Sam Altman gave 1,000 people $1,000 a month for three years. They worked about 1.3 hours less per week and spent the money mostly on basics like food, housing and transport.10

So, roughly: people given money don't stop working, don't buy yachts, and pay their rent. That's useful to know. But these experiments answer the question "what happens if some people get a cheque in an economy that still runs on work?". The question AI is asking is the opposite one.

09The question behind the question

If I had to boil this down to one idea, it's this: the question isn't whether there will be jobs. It's who owns the machines.

For two hundred years, most people got their share of the economy through wages. Wages were how the pie got sliced. If AI makes labour much less valuable, the pie can still be huge; it just stops being sliced through work. What's left is ownership: of companies, of data, of compute, of energy, of land. If you own a piece of that, automation is a dividend. If you don't, it's a layoff letter.

That's why the more serious proposals, whatever you think of them, end up being about ownership rather than jobs. A pension fund that holds AI companies turns workers into the robots' landlords. A sovereign fund turns a country into a shareholder. Neither is utopian. Norway has been doing a version of it with oil for decades. Which, given the previous section, is the most encouraging thing about petrostates I'll ever write.

Reuther's question was never really about cars. It was about who the economy is for. For most of history, the answer was forced on everyone by a simple fact: you couldn't run a company without people. That fact is getting weaker, and when it's gone, the answer will be whatever we decide it is. I'd rather we decide it on purpose, before the machines are doing all the work and we're left arguing about it in our extra free time. Keynes would at least be pleased about that part.
