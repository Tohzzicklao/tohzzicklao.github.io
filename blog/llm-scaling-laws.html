Bigger is predictable: a short history of LLM scaling laws

August 12, 2026 · José Antonio Nicolás Navarro

Most of machine learning is empirical and messy. You change something, retrain, and hope. Scaling laws are the rare exception: a handful of curves that let you predict, before spending a single GPU-hour, roughly how good a model will be if you make it bigger, feed it more data or train it longer.

Those curves are why labs were willing to spend millions, and then billions, on single training runs. They have also been revised, disputed and partially rewritten every couple of years, which is a lot for something called a law. This post walks through that story, paper by paper: what each law said, what it got wrong, and what it changed in practice.

01Before the transformers: 2017

The idea is older than GPT. In December 2017, a team at Baidu Research led by Joel Hestness trained models across four domains (machine translation, language modeling, image classification and speech recognition) on growing slices of data. In every one of them, generalization error fell as a power law of dataset size. Better architectures shifted the curve down, but didn't seem to change its slope. Model size, in turn, needed to grow only sublinearly with data.1

That last observation is the important one. If the exponent is a property of the problem and not of your clever architecture, then progress is less about ideas and more about budget. Rich Sutton made the same point more bluntly in March 2019, in an essay every ML engineer eventually gets sent: across 70 years of AI research, general methods that leverage computation have ended up beating hand-crafted knowledge "by a large margin".2 He called it the bitter lesson. Mostly bitter for the people who had spent careers hand-crafting that knowledge.

02Kaplan et al., 2020: the power laws

The paper that turned the intuition into a recipe came out of OpenAI in January 2020. Jared Kaplan and colleagues (several of whom would go on to found Anthropic) trained hundreds of transformer language models and measured one thing: cross-entropy loss on held-out text. They found that it follows smooth power laws in three variables, as long as the other two aren't the bottleneck:3

L(N) ∝ N−0.076  ·  L(D) ∝ D−0.095  ·  L(C) ∝ C−0.050 N = parameters (non-embedding) · D = training tokens · C = training compute

The exponents look tiny, and they are. A loss that scales with C−0.05 needs roughly 10× more compute to fall by about 11%. But the striking part wasn't the size of the gains, it was their regularity: straight lines on a log-log plot over more than six orders of magnitude. Width, depth and number of heads barely mattered within a broad range. Scale did.3

Kaplan's team also asked the practical question: given a fixed compute budget, how should you split it between a bigger model and more data? Their answer was that large models are much more sample-efficient, so the optimal strategy was to train very large models on comparatively modest data and stop well before convergence.3 In their fits, the optimal model size grew roughly as N ∝ C0.73: most of each extra unit of compute should go into parameters.

Four months later, OpenAI released GPT-3: 175 billion parameters, trained on about 300 billion tokens.4 That's fewer than two tokens per parameter: a very large model that had skimmed everything roughly once. Within Kaplan's framework, it was a perfectly reasonable bet. And for two years, the industry made the same one: Gopher, Jurassic-1 and Megatron-Turing NLG all pushed parameter counts into the hundreds of billions while training data stayed around the same few hundred billion tokens.

03Chinchilla, 2022: everyone was undertrained

In March 2022, DeepMind's Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues published what everyone now calls the Chinchilla paper. Its abstract contains one of the more consequential sentences in recent ML: "We find that current large language models are significantly undertrained".5 Not the most comfortable sentence to write when one of those models is Gopher, which DeepMind had trained itself a few months earlier.

They trained over 400 models, from 70 million to more than 16 billion parameters, on 5 to 500 billion tokens, and estimated the compute-optimal frontier in three different ways. All three pointed the same direction: model size and training tokens should scale in equal proportion. Double the compute, and you should make the model about 1.4× bigger and feed it about 1.4× more data. That works out to a rule of thumb that stuck: around 20 tokens per parameter.5

One of their three methods fits a parametric loss that I find the most intuitive way to think about scaling:

L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ E ≈ 1.69 · A ≈ 406.4 · B ≈ 410.7 · α ≈ 0.34 · β ≈ 0.28 (Hoffmann et al.)

Read it from left to right. E is the irreducible loss: the entropy of natural text itself, which no model can beat. The second term is what you lose by having a finite model, the third what you lose by having finite data. Scaling only one of them leaves the other term untouched, which is exactly what the GPT-3 generation had done: a bigger engine every year, same tank of fuel.

The proof was the model itself. Chinchilla had 70B parameters, a quarter of Gopher's 280B, and was trained on about four times more data with the same compute budget, roughly 1.4 trillion tokens. It beat Gopher, GPT-3, Jurassic-1 and Megatron-Turing NLG on a wide range of tasks, reaching 67.5% on MMLU, more than 7 points above Gopher. And because it was smaller, it was also cheaper to fine-tune and serve.5

04Why Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreed

Two careful teams, similar methods, very different answers (N ∝ C0.73 versus N ∝ C0.5). Science is hard, and harder when every data point costs a GPU cluster. For a while the usual explanation was learning-rate schedules: Kaplan's runs didn't adapt the schedule to the number of training tokens, which penalised the longer runs.

In 2024, Tim Pearce and Jinyeop Song offered a cleaner account. Kaplan counted only non-embedding parameters, and fitted mostly at small scale, where embeddings are a big share of the model. Simulating the Chinchilla study under those same conditions reproduces coefficients close to Kaplan's. Their conclusion: Kaplan overestimated how fast parameters should grow, and Chinchilla's coefficients stand.6

Chinchilla didn't get a free pass either. In April 2024, Tamay Besiroglu and colleagues at Epoch AI tried to replicate the parametric fit by extracting the data from the paper's plots. They found the published estimates inconsistent with the paper's own first two methods, with implausibly narrow confidence intervals; their refit agreed with those two methods instead.7 The headline recommendation survived. The exact numbers in the formula above are best treated as illustrative. Which is a polite way of saying I plotted them anyway.

The meta-lesson is a bit uncomfortable: scaling laws are empirical fits, and small choices in what you count and how you fit can move the "optimal" answer by an order of magnitude at frontier scale.

05Emergence, or a trick of the metric?

Smooth loss curves raised an obvious question: if loss improves gradually, why do some capabilities seem to switch on suddenly? In June 2022, Jason Wei and colleagues catalogued dozens of tasks, like multi-step arithmetic, where small models scored at chance and larger ones jumped well above it. They called these abilities emergent: not present in smaller models, present in larger ones, and therefore not predictable by extrapolating from the small ones.8

In April 2023, Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda and Sanmi Koyejo pushed back. Many of those jumps, they argued, come from the metric, not the model. Exact-match accuracy on a five-digit answer is zero until every digit is right, so a model that is steadily getting better per token looks flat and then suddenly "gets it". Like a student who fails every exam by one digit until, one day, they don't. Switch to a continuous metric and the same outputs improve smoothly.9

I think both papers are right about something. The underlying competence does tend to scale smoothly. But users and attackers don't experience per-token log-likelihoods; they experience whether the exploit works or the answer is correct. From the outside, thresholds are real, even if the curve beneath them is a straight line.

06Beyond Chinchilla: training for inference

Chinchilla answers a specific question: what's the best model for a fixed training budget? But a model that's going to serve millions of users spends far more compute on inference than it ever did in training, and inference cost scales with parameter count. Under that constraint, a smaller model trained for much longer can be the better deal, even if it's "inefficient" by Chinchilla's standard.

Nikhil Sardana and colleagues formalised this at the end of 2023: if you expect high inference demand, you should train models smaller and longer than Chinchilla-optimal, and in their experiments quality kept improving at ratios of up to 10,000 tokens per parameter.10 Meta's Llama 3, released in April 2024, is the poster child. Both the 8B and 70B models were trained on over 15 trillion tokens. Meta noted that the Chinchilla-optimal amount for the 8B model would have been around 200 billion tokens, and that performance kept improving log-linearly far beyond it.11 Chinchilla would have called that a waste of compute. Whoever pays the inference bill calls it a bargain.

Chinchilla wasn't wrong here; it was answering a different question. The industry just moved from asking "what's the best model I can train?" to "what's the best model I can afford to run?".

07The data wall

Overtraining, though, makes another problem worse. If every model wants trillions of tokens, and data has to grow as fast as parameters, sooner or later you run out of internet. It's finite; it just never felt that way. Epoch AI's Pablo Villalobos and colleagues estimated that, at current trends, models would use up the effective stock of public human-written text somewhere between 2026 and 2032, or slightly earlier if models are heavily overtrained.12 We are, as I write this, inside that window.

Compute, meanwhile, keeps growing. Epoch's tracking puts the training compute of notable models growing around 4–5× per year since 2010, with bigger training clusters as the main driver since 2018.13 Data doesn't grow anywhere near that fast.

There are partial escape hatches. Niklas Muennighoff and colleagues showed that repeating data for up to about 4 epochs is nearly as good as fresh data for a fixed compute budget, but that returns fade to zero with much more repetition.14 Synthetic data, better filtering and multimodal data all help at the margins. None of them changes the basic picture, which Ilya Sutskever summed up in his NeurIPS talk in December 2024: compute keeps growing, but there is only one internet, and "pre-training as we know it will end".15 He also called data the fossil fuel of AI, which is a bleak metaphor if you remember how things went with the other one.

08A new axis: test-time compute

If you can't make pre-training much bigger, scale something else. If the model can't get bigger, let it think longer: a strategy also popular with students during exams. In August 2024, Charlie Snell and colleagues showed that letting a model spend more compute at inference time (searching against a verifier, or revising its own answers) could be allocated optimally per prompt. With that strategy, in a FLOPs-matched comparison, a smaller model could outperform a 14× larger one on problems where it already had a reasonable chance of succeeding.16

A month later OpenAI released o1, trained with reinforcement learning to produce long chains of reasoning before answering. OpenAI reported that its performance improved consistently both with more RL during training and with more time spent thinking at test time: two new scaling curves, on top of the old one.17 In January 2025, DeepSeek-R1 showed that this kind of reasoning could be incentivised through pure reinforcement learning, without human-labelled reasoning traces, with self-reflection and verification behaviours appearing along the way.18

So the single scaling law of 2020 has become at least three, each with its own cost structure:

AxisWhat you scalePaidMain limit
Pre-trainingParameters and tokensOnce, up frontHuman-written data
Post-training (RL)Reinforcement learning on verifiable tasksOnce, up frontTasks with checkable answers
Test-timeReasoning tokens, samples, searchOn every queryLatency and inference cost

The economics shifted again. Pre-training compute is a one-off cost amortised across every user. Test-time compute is billed per question, which is why the same model can now be cheap and fast or slow and expensive, depending on how hard you let it think. Thinking, it turns out, is billed by the token.

09Scaling what we actually care about

Loss is a convenient thing to scale, but nobody buys a model for its perplexity. The more interesting recent question is whether useful capability also follows a trend. METR's attempt is my favourite: measure the length of tasks, in human working time, that a model can complete with 50% reliability. Across models since 2019, that horizon has been doubling roughly every seven months, possibly faster since 2024.19

It's a different kind of scaling law: it doesn't separate parameters, data and compute, it just tracks the frontier over calendar time. But it's the one that matters most for people like me working on security. When the length of tasks an agent can complete keeps doubling, so does the length of an attack chain it can run without a human in the loop, and the length of the defensive workflow we can hand to one.

10What actually held up

Looking back over almost a decade, the specific numbers have aged badly. Kaplan's exponents were biased, Chinchilla's coefficients were disputed, the 20-tokens-per-parameter rule is ignored by nearly every production model, and pre-training is no longer the only curve worth climbing. What held up is the shape: performance improves predictably and with diminishing returns as you spend more compute, and the question is always where the next unit of compute buys the most.

In 2020 the answer was parameters. In 2022 it was data. In 2024 it was smaller models trained for longer, and then reasoning at inference time. Each shift came from someone asking the same question with a different constraint in mind: a fixed training budget, an inference bill, a finite internet.

That's why I find scaling laws more interesting than any single model. They're not laws of nature; they're accounting. They tell you what a capability will cost, and the field keeps finding new places to spend the money. Whatever the next axis turns out to be, my bet is that someone will plot it on a log-log chart, find a straight line, and start a very expensive training run.
