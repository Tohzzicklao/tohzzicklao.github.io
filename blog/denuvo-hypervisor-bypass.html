Lying from Ring -1: the Denuvo Hypervisor Bypass

October 2, 2026 | José Antonio Nicolás Navarro

For about a decade, Denuvo ran on a simple and honestly rather elegant business model: make cracking a game so tedious that, by the time someone finished, everyone who wanted it had already bought it. Crackers spent months untangling obfuscated code. Publishers enjoyed their launch window in peace. Everybody knew their role.

Then, in 2026, someone stopped asking "how do I break Denuvo?" and started asking a much ruder question: what if I just lie to it? From underneath Windows, where it can't check.

Before anyone gets ideas: this is an educational analysis. Everything in it comes from information already publicly available on the internet (press coverage, public analyses and academic papers), all linked in the sources at the bottom. I haven't used or analysed the bypass myself, and this post isn't affiliated with or endorsed by Irdeto or Denuvo. No tools, no download links, no instructions. I'm interested in the security engineering, not in your Steam library. Buy the games.

01Denuvo in thirty seconds

Denuvo Anti-Tamper, owned by Irdeto, is not the kind of DRM that checks your license once and goes back to sleep. It gets woven into the game binary itself: chunks of code are transformed and virtualized (translated into instructions for a made-up CPU that only Denuvo's own interpreter understands), and integrity checks, the so-called triggers, are sprinkled through the game and fire while you play.

On the license side, according to Wikipedia, each copy is activated online and receives a token bound to a fingerprint of your machine, built from hardware and system properties. If the fingerprint doesn't match, the game doesn't run. That's why swapping your CPU or updating your BIOS can make Denuvo decide you're a stranger.1 From its point of view, you kind of are.

Classic cracking meant reverse engineering all of that by hand, game by game, trigger by trigger. Weeks to months of work. And that was the product. Denuvo never really promised "uncrackable". It promised "not this month", and priced itself accordingly.

02A hypervisor, briefly

Modern CPUs run code at different privilege levels, called rings. Your applications live in Ring 3, the least trusted: they can't touch the hardware or other programs' memory directly, and have to ask the operating system for almost everything. The Windows kernel lives in Ring 0, with full control of the machine. On top of that, CPUs come with hardware virtualization (Intel VT-x, AMD-V), which lets a hypervisor run whole operating systems as guests, each one convinced it has the machine to itself. The hypervisor sits at what people call Ring -1, below the kernel, and code at one level can't see or overrule what happens below it. If the kernel is the landlord, the hypervisor owns the building. The landlord believes he's in charge. He is mistaken.

The relevant trick is that a hypervisor can tell the CPU: "whenever the guest runs this instruction, stop and ask me first". That's a VM exit. Control jumps to the hypervisor, which looks at the request, decides what the guest gets to see, and quietly resumes it (a VM entry). The textbook example is CPUID, the instruction software uses to ask the processor who and what it is. From inside the guest, nothing looks different. That's the whole point of virtualization. As it turns out, it's also the whole point of this bypass.

03Don't pick the lock, move the house

The best-known name here is DenuvOwO (yes, really), a cracker who published their hypervisor as open source. According to XDA Developers, instead of patching the game, the approach slides a thin custom hypervisor underneath an already running Windows, no reboot required.4 The game itself stays untouched, triggers and all. Denuvo keeps asking the machine its usual questions. It's just that the machine answering is no longer the machine.

I find this genuinely beautiful from an engineering perspective. The fingerprinting code runs. The validation runs. Everything works exactly as designed, on fabricated inputs. Nobody breaks the cryptography; they simply control the layer the defender uses to observe reality.

The idea wasn't new, either. In January 2025, reverse engineer Connor-Jay Dunn closed a detailed analysis of Denuvo by suggesting that a hypervisor could spoof all the hardware information it relies on, while warning that faking the ntdll (the lowest layer of user-mode Windows, the library that actually hands requests to the kernel) and KUSER_SHARED_DATA (more on that below) checks without side effects would be the hard part.12 His write-up also lists CPUID, raw syscalls and KUSER_SHARED_DATA among the fingerprint sources, which is exactly the list of things the bypass ended up lying about.

And judging by the public reports, because the trick doesn't depend on any particular game's code, it's largely generic: once it works for a given Denuvo version, it tends to work for every game shipping that version. That's the part that should keep Irdeto up at night. They built a per-game problem, and someone came back with a per-version solution. According to Wikipedia's timeline, day-one releases of protected games, something nobody had seen since 2018, suddenly became routine.1

04What exactly is being lied about

According to XDA Developers' analysis, the full stack touches every privilege level of the machine. At the bottom, an optional boot-time component switches off PatchGuard (Windows' watchdog, which crashes the system if someone modifies core kernel structures), Driver Signature Enforcement (which only lets properly signed drivers load) and VBS (more on that in section 06) before Windows even loads, so an unsigned hypervisor can get in and kernel structures can be edited without anyone complaining. At the top, a Steam emulator (a stand-in for the library games use to talk to Steam) handles the ownership questions, which is the most boring part of the whole thing and a problem the scene solved years ago.4 The interesting work happens in the middle, at Ring -1, and it's mostly a list of questions Denuvo is no longer allowed to get honest answers to.

The first is CPUID, the processor's ID card: vendor, model, feature flags. It's a core ingredient of the hardware fingerprint, and it can also reveal that a hypervisor is running. According to XDA, the bypass makes it describe the CPU the license token expects, with no hypervisor in sight.4 Lying about whether you're lying: a classic.

Then there's timing. VM exits aren't free; Xen developer Keir Fraser estimated each one at around a thousand cycles7, and a program that times instructions which should be instant, by reading the CPU's cycle counter with RDTSC before and after, can notice the detour. So, according to XDA, the bypass also hides that detour from the clock. If the clock says nothing happened, nothing happened.4

Next, KUSER_SHARED_DATA: a read-only page Windows maps at a fixed address in every process, holding system time, OS version and processor feature details. Protections love it because they can read it without calling a single API, which makes it resistant to hooking. A great property right up until whoever lives below the operating system decides to edit it, which, according to crackrelease.com's write-up, is exactly what happens.5

A syscall is how a program asks the kernel to do something for it, like opening a file or reporting system information. Normally that request passes through several layers of Windows libraries, and any of them can be hooked to return a fake answer. So protections like to skip the Windows API and make direct syscalls, talking to the kernel without going through anything that could be hooked in user mode. Very sensible. Unfortunately, the hypervisor is not a user-mode hook, and according to the same write-up it sees those transitions anyway.5

And finally SGDT, an old instruction that reveals where the CPU's global descriptor table (one of the tables the kernel sets up at boot to describe memory to the processor) lives in memory. It's a sibling of SIDT, the instruction behind Joanna Rutkowska's "Red Pill", one of the first VM detection tricks, from around 2004 and now old enough to rent a car.8,9 The idea behind Red Pill was simple: early virtual machines had to keep these tables at different addresses than a real machine would, so just reading the address told you where you were. Values like these differ between real and virtualized machines, so SGDT works as both a fingerprint input and a hypervisor detector, and, per the same write-up, it gets emulated too.5

None of this is exotic. These are the standard ways a program asks "where am I?". The bypass just makes sure the answer is always "exactly where you expected".

05Meanwhile, on Linux

According to Wikipedia and crackrelease.com, in mid-2026 a developer going by LinUwUx (the naming convention in this scene is, let's say, consistent) ported the approach to Linux, using DenuvOwO's open-source hypervisor as a reference.1,5 GameGPU reports a claim of 173 working games, Steam Deck included.6 And the funny part is how little of it needs a hypervisor at all.

Think about it: on Linux, the game is already running inside a fake Windows. Proton, the compatibility layer Valve ships with Steam, is built on Wine, which runs Windows programs by reimplementing the Windows API on top of Linux. Wine, with all due respect to the people who've spent decades building it, is a very elaborate and very well-intentioned lie. According to crackrelease.com, the bypass just adds a few more lines to the script, from inside the game's own process:

CheckWindows (hypervisor)Linux (Proton/Wine)
CPUIDTrapped via VM exit at Ring -1CPUID faulting, a documented CPU feature that makes CPUID trap instead of answering, so the answer can come from inside the process.
KUSER_SHARED_DATAEdited from below the OSWine emulates this page itself.
Direct syscallsSeen by the hypervisorLinux's Syscall User Dispatch, a kernel feature that sends a process's syscalls to a handler inside that same process, which Wine already uses for games that make raw Windows syscalls.
SGDTEmulated by the hypervisorThe weak spot: a Linux hardening feature (UMIP, which blocks SGDT and similar instructions in regular programs) gets in the way.5

CPUID faulting is the piece that decides whether you still need a hypervisor. It's a documented CPU feature that Linux exposes per process. As rr's author Robert O'Callahan explained, it was originally added in kernel 4.12 for something deeply unglamorous: the rr record-and-replay debugger, which records a program's execution so it can be replayed exactly, and needs to control what CPUID returns for the replay to match.10 It's now enjoying an unexpected second career. According to crackrelease.com, recent Intel CPUs have it, and so does AMD from Zen 4 with a recent kernel. Older Ryzen chips, Steam Deck included, don't, and reportedly still rely on a hypervisor to fake it.5 When it's available, executing CPUID makes the kernel send the process a signal (Linux's way of interrupting a program to tell it something happened, here SIGSEGV), and a handler registered inside the Wine process catches it and supplies the answer.

That contrast is the real takeaway. On Windows, telling a convincing lie meant going below the kernel and switching off half the platform's security. On Linux it mostly fits into extension points that already exist: a signal handler, a memory page Wine owns anyway, a syscall dispatcher. Cheaper, though not free: you're still running unofficial Proton builds, and on some machines turning off a kernel hardening feature.

There's also a slightly bitter irony here. The people most likely to pay for all this are Linux players with perfectly legitimate copies. If Denuvo's answer is to lean harder on Windows kernel features that Wine can't faithfully reproduce, games that run fine on Proton today may stop running tomorrow, for the people who actually bought them.4

06The bill, please

On Windows, none of this is free. As The FPS Review reported, to load a homemade hypervisor under the kernel, you have to switch off Secure Boot, Virtualization-Based Security and friends: the exact features Microsoft spent the better part of a decade begging everyone to turn on. Secure Boot is what stops untrusted code from running before Windows starts. VBS and HVCI use Microsoft's own hypervisor to protect the kernel, and they also happen to occupy the very seat the bypass wants to sit in. So they have to go.2

What you're left with is a machine where the most privileged code running is a binary someone downloaded from a forum, sitting below the OS and below any antivirus that might have had an opinion about it. Joanna Rutkowska demonstrated exactly this scenario as a hypothetical rootkit (malware built to hide from the operating system and the security tools running on it), Blue Pill, back in 2006.11 Twenty years later, people are installing it voluntarily to save seventy euros. That's a bold risk assessment.

Tellingly, PCWorld reported that some crackers refuse to use the method at all.3 When the people with the least respect for DRM on the planet think the cure is worse than the disease, it's worth listening. And as a bonus, per the same article, it can break anti-cheat systems that require Secure Boot, and cause performance or compatibility issues on some CPUs.3

07The arms race

Irdeto's public response, to TorrentFreak, was that it's "already working on updated security versions for games impacted by hypervisor bypasses" and that performance won't suffer.2 It also reportedly said the fix won't involve Denuvo itself moving to Ring -1 or deeper into the kernel,2 which is reassuring, because the only thing worse than a DRM in your kernel would be a DRM in your hypervisor. According to Wikipedia, in September 2026 the company also took the legal route and filed a DMCA anti-circumvention lawsuit (under the US law that makes bypassing copy protection illegal in itself) against one of the people behind the releases.1

Technically, the options for spotting a hidden hypervisor are well known, and none of them are great. Checking CPUID for a hypervisor is, as we've seen, asking the liar if he's lying. Timing checks work in theory, but the hypervisor can compensate the clock and real hardware is noisy enough to make thresholds fragile. Requiring Secure Boot or VBS to be on would work, at the cost of locking out Linux, the Steam Deck and plenty of older setups. More frequent online validation works too, and punishes exactly the customers who paid. Every option moves the cost onto someone, and historically that someone has been the person with the receipt.

08What defenders should take from this

The obvious lesson is the oldest one in the book: the client is not yours. Any check that runs on hardware the attacker controls can eventually be fed false inputs. Anti-tamper buys time; it doesn't buy guarantees. Denuvo always knew this. The surprise was how much time the hypervisor approach took off the clock.

The more interesting lesson is about where attacks land. The best bypasses rarely beat your algorithm. They control what it sees: emulators, hooked APIs, spoofed sensors, and in this case an entirely fictional CPU. If your security model assumes the environment is honest, the environment is your attack surface. And in that fight, whoever sits lower in the stack wins, whether we're talking about DRM, anti-cheat, EDR or malware.

Finally, security trade-offs are contagious. A bypass that requires turning off Secure Boot and VBS doesn't just defeat a DRM. It downgrades the security of the whole machine, for everything else that runs on it, for as long as it stays that way.

What I like about this whole story, as an engineer, is that the bypass isn't clever about its target. It's clever about where it stands. The cheapest way past a security check is rarely to defeat it. It's to make it believe in a different world, and then let it do its job perfectly.
