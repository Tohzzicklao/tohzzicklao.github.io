Half a second too slow: the xz-utils backdoor

July 7, 2026 · José Antonio Nicolás Navarro

In March 2024, a database developer noticed that his SSH logins were taking about half a second longer than they should. Most of us would have blamed the Wi-Fi. He started digging instead, and ended up exposing what may be the most patient and carefully engineered supply chain attack ever made public.

The target wasn't a company or a government. It was a compression library maintained, for most of its life, by one volunteer. The weapon wasn't an exploit. It was two years of being helpful, and a few well-placed emails about how slow things were.

Before anyone gets ideas: this is an educational analysis. Everything in it comes from information already publicly available (the original disclosure, public timelines, the maintainer's statements and public technical analyses), all linked in the sources at the bottom. I haven't analysed the backdoor binary myself, and the technical parts stay at the level of how it worked, not how to rebuild it. The interesting part, honestly, isn't the code anyway. It's the people.

01The most boring critical software you've never thought about

XZ Utils is a data compression package. Its library, liblzma, is installed on essentially every Linux distribution, because package managers, kernels and countless tools need to decompress things. It's the kind of software that only gets noticed when it breaks, which is to say: never.

For years it was maintained by Lasse Collin, largely on his own, in his free time. If you've seen XKCD's "Dependency" comic, the one where all of modern digital infrastructure rests on a tiny block labelled as a project some random person has been thanklessly maintaining since 2003, you already know the shape of this story.7 Someone else had clearly seen it too.

02Act I: a helpful stranger

On October 29, 2021, a contributor calling themselves Jia Tan sent their first patch to the xz mailing list. It added an .editorconfig file. A month later came a fix for a reproducible build problem. Then more small, competent, entirely legitimate patches.2

That's the part that makes this attack so unsettling. For a long time, Jia Tan wasn't pretending to be a good contributor. Jia Tan was a good contributor. The kind every overworked maintainer dreams of: reliable, polite, low-maintenance. Exactly what you'd build if you were building a key to someone else's house.

03Act II: the pressure campaign

In April 2022, a new name appeared on the list. "Jigar Kumar" complained that patches weren't landing: "Patches spend years on this mailing list." A month later: "Over 1 month and no closer to being merged." In June: "Progress will not happen until there is new maintainer."2 Another account, "Dennis Ens", joined in asking whether the project was even maintained. Neither had much of a history before or after. Both are widely believed to have been sock puppets.2,3

On June 8, 2022, Collin replied with a level of honesty that is hard to read in hindsight:

"I haven't lost interest but my ability to care has been fairly limited mostly due to longterm mental health issues but also due to some other things." Lasse Collin, xz-devel, June 20222

In the same message, he mentioned that Jia Tan had been helping him off-list and might have a bigger role in the future. Two weeks later, Dennis Ens wrote back: "I am sorry about your mental health issues, but its important to be aware of your own limits."2 Sympathy and pressure in a single sentence. By June 29, Collin was describing Jia Tan as "practically a co-maintainer already".

I work on detecting social engineering for a living, and this is a textbook case. No urgency about money, no suspicious link. Just a manufactured problem (the project is too slow), a manufactured crowd (several voices saying so), and a convenient solution already standing in the room. It works on people in finance departments, and it works on open source maintainers. It works best on people who are tired.

What followed was a slow, methodical accumulation of control. Each step was small and plausible on its own; it only looks sinister when you line them up:2

There's even a small extra: in February 2024, a subtle typo in a configure check quietly broke detection of Landlock, a Linux sandboxing feature.2 Not part of the backdoor itself. Just a bit of tidying up the neighbourhood before moving in.

05Hiding in plain sight

On February 23, 2024, the payload went in. Not as code, but as two binary files in the test suite, with reassuringly boring names like bad-3-corrupt_lzma2.xz.1,2 A compression library is supposed to have corrupt compressed files in its tests. Nobody reviews binary test fixtures byte by byte. As Andres Freund later noted, they weren't even used by any test in 5.6.0.1

The other half never touched the git repository at all. The release tarballs for 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 shipped a modified m4/build-to-host.m4, an autotools file that is normally generated and that nobody reads. During the build, it extracted the payload from the test files and spliced it into liblzma, but only under very specific conditions: x86-64 Linux, built with gcc and the GNU linker, as part of a Debian or RPM package build.1 In other words: exactly where distributions build the packages that end up on servers, and nowhere a curious developer would casually look.

5.6.0 was released on February 24, 2024. Shortly after, Red Hat-based systems started seeing Valgrind errors in liblzma. Jia Tan responded the way any diligent maintainer would: with a fix. Version 5.6.1, released on March 9, quietly updated the backdoor so it would stop tripping Valgrind.2 The backdoor got a bug fix release. Some legitimate projects can only dream of that level of maintenance.

06Why SSH?

Here's the clever bit. OpenSSH doesn't use liblzma. But several distributions, including Debian and Fedora, patch sshd to notify systemd when it starts, and that pulls in libsystemd, which does depend on liblzma.1 So a compression library ends up loaded inside the process that guards remote access to the machine. Nobody designed that. It just happened, one reasonable dependency at a time.

Once inside sshd, the backdoor used the ifunc mechanism added the year before to run code very early during loading, and redirected RSA_public_decrypt, a function OpenSSH uses while checking keys during login, to its own code.1 It also checked its environment carefully before doing anything: it only activated when running as /usr/sbin/sshd and seemed to avoid debuggers.1

Public analyses of the payload later showed what it was for. A client could hide an encrypted command inside the key of an SSH certificate. If, and only if, that command carried a valid signature from the attacker's Ed448 key, tied to the specific server's host key, the backdoor executed it with system(), before any authentication and as root.5 Anyone else got a perfectly normal login.

That last detail is what makes it professional. This wasn't a hole anyone could use; it was a door with exactly one key. Scanners wouldn't find it, other attackers couldn't hijack it, and without the private key you couldn't even prove it did anything. As Alex Stamos put it, it "could have been the most widespread and effective backdoor ever planted in any software product".3

07Half a second

And then it was all undone by a benchmark. Andres Freund, a PostgreSQL developer working at Microsoft, had been noticing "a few odd symptoms" on his Debian unstable machines for weeks: SSH logins using a lot of CPU, and Valgrind complaining about liblzma. He measured it. A failed SSH login that used to take 0.299s now took 0.807s.1 He dug in, found the injected build script, and on March 29, 2024, posted to the oss-security mailing list:

"The upstream xz repository and the xz tarballs have been backdoored." Andres Freund, oss-security, March 29, 20241

In the same post, he described himself as "*not* a security researcher, nor a reverse engineer".1 Which is, frankly, a little humbling for those of us who are.

The timing was tight. In the weeks before, Jia Tan and "Hans Jansen" had been asking distributions to pick up the new versions: Fedora 40 in late February, Debian in March, Ubuntu on March 28, the day before the disclosure.2 The backdoor had reached Debian testing and unstable, Fedora Rawhide and the Fedora 40 beta, but not the stable releases that run most servers.2,3 Canonical delayed the Ubuntu 24.04 beta to rebuild everything from scratch.3 Meanwhile, a systemd change that stopped linking liblzma into libsystemd, which would have defeated this particular route into sshd, had been merged weeks earlier.2 The attackers were racing an unrelated refactor. They lost to a stopwatch.

It received CVE-2024-3094, with a CVSS score of 10.0, the maximum.3 Collin regained control of the project, removed Jia Tan's access and published clean releases in May 2024.3,4 For the record, in August 2025 researchers at Binarly still found the backdoor inside some Debian images on Docker Hub, which Debian declined to remove, calling them historical artifacts.3 Technically accurate. The best kind of accurate.

08Who was Jia Tan?

Nobody knows, at least not publicly. The name, the time zones in the commits and the accounts around it have all been picked apart, and none of it leads anywhere solid, which is itself a kind of answer. Two-plus years of patient cover, multiple coordinated personas and a payload built to be used by exactly one party is not how hobbyists work. Dave Aitel suggested the pattern fits APT29, linked to Russia's SVR; others argued it could be any capable state or a well-resourced group.3 I'm not going to pretend I know better.

What we do know is that it wasn't a one-off idea. In April 2024, the OpenSSF and OpenJS Foundation warned that the xz case "may not be an isolated incident", after OpenJS received a series of suspicious emails from different names urging it to make unfamiliar people maintainers of a popular JavaScript project, to fix unspecified "critical vulnerabilities". They saw a similar pattern in two other projects.6 Same playbook, less patience.

09What defenders should take from this

The attack surface was a person. Every technical step depended on Jia Tan first becoming trusted, and that trust came from a social engineering campaign aimed at someone who was overworked and honest about it. Code review, signing and CI all worked as designed. They just trusted the wrong maintainer.

What you review must be what you build. Half the backdoor lived in a release tarball that didn't match the repository. Reproducible builds, building from tagged source instead of uploaded archives, and treating binary blobs in a repository as code all close that gap. The OpenSSF's list of warning signs reads like a summary of this story: unknown people pushing hard for maintainer status, endorsements from other unknown accounts, binary blobs in pull requests, changes to build and release practices, and manufactured urgency.6

Critical infrastructure needs to be funded like critical infrastructure. A single tired volunteer was the last line of defence for SSH on a large share of Linux servers. That's not a failure of the volunteer. It's a failure of everyone who depended on his work without ever asking how he was doing, until someone with very different intentions did.

And finally: luck is not a strategy. This was caught because one person cared about half a second, at the right time, on the right distribution. It's worth asking how many similar attempts didn't add any latency.

What stays with me about xz isn't the cleverness of the payload, although it's clever. It's that the most sophisticated part of the operation was kindness: a reliable contributor, a sympathetic tone, an offer to help. Most security controls are designed to stop strangers. This one was stopped by a stranger, too, just a different one, who happened to be in a hurry to log in.
