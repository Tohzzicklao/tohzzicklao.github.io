Inside Denuvo: how the most hated DRM actually works

September 10, 2026 | José Antonio Nicolás Navarro

Ask a PC gamer what Denuvo does and you'll usually get one of two answers: "it kills performance" or "it gets cracked anyway". Ask them how it works and you'll mostly get silence. That's not their fault. Irdeto has never published the internals, and the people who know them best have every reason to stay quiet.

Still, over the years a handful of reverse engineers have written up what they found, carefully and without handing out tools. Put together, those write-ups describe something more interesting than "DRM": a system that turns your hardware into part of the program.

Before anyone gets ideas: this is an educational analysis. Everything in it comes from information already publicly available on the internet (researchers' write-ups, press coverage and an academic paper), all linked in the sources at the bottom. I haven't reverse engineered Denuvo myself, and this post isn't affiliated with or endorsed by Irdeto or Denuvo. No tools, no download links, no step-by-step anything. I'm interested in the software protection engineering, not in your Steam library. Buy the games.

01Anti-tamper, not DRM

First, a distinction that matters. Denuvo Anti-Tamper doesn't decide whether you own a game. Steam (or another store) does that. Denuvo's job is to make it very expensive to rip out or fake the code that asks that question. It's the armour around the lock, not the lock.

The company has a fittingly DRM-flavoured family tree. According to its Wikipedia entry, Denuvo Software Solutions was formed in Salzburg through a management buyout of DigitalWorks, the Sony DADC unit that built SecuROM, the copy protection many of us remember from the 2000s with mixed feelings. Its first game was FIFA 15, in September 2014.1 In January 2018 Irdeto, a digital security company better known for protecting pay-TV, bought it along with its 45 employees.1,4

02The license: a token cut to your machine

According to Maurice Heumann, who reverse engineered the Denuvo build in Hogwarts Legacy (took the compiled game apart to work out what the protection does, without access to its source code), the flow looks like this. The first time you launch a protected game, it collects a set of hardware and software properties into a fingerprint and asks Steam for a ticket proving you own the game. Both go to Denuvo's servers, which check the ticket and send back a token that only works on a machine with that exact fingerprint.3 The token is cached, so you don't need to be online every time you play; Connor-Jay Dunn calls it a "semi-online DRM", which is about right.2

The fingerprint is also why Denuvo has activation limits. According to Wikipedia, each license allows five devices in a 24-hour window, and swapping a CPU or motherboard, or even a big OS or BIOS update, can make your PC look like a new device and burn another slot.1 Annoying, but it follows directly from the design: the token isn't "for you", it's for a very specific description of your computer.

So far this sounds like ordinary licensing. The clever part is what the token actually contains.

03Stolen constants: your PC is the decryption key

A naive DRM would check the token once and branch: valid, play; invalid, quit. That's one if, and crackers have been flipping single ifs since the 1980s. Denuvo doesn't really have that branch. According to Dunn's analysis, it removes pieces of the game's own code and makes the token the only way to get them back.2

Take an instruction like mov dword ptr [rbp-4], edi. If assembly isn't your thing: it copies a value from a CPU register (edi) into a local variable stored 4 bytes below a reference point on the stack (rbp). The -4 is chosen by the compiler and never changes afterwards, so it can be cut out of the binary entirely. The function is moved into Denuvo's virtual machine (more on that in section 05), and the missing value is stored server-side, mixed with your hardware identifiers through reversible math. That mixture is what ends up in your license. At runtime, the VM reads your hardware again, undoes the math and recovers -4, then executes the original instruction.2 Heumann, from the other side, saw the same thing: the token is used "to e.g. decrypt certain values at runtime".3 Dunn adds that after the first activation the license is kept as a local file, and that before handing control to the game's original entry point (OEP: the spot where the game's own code would start running if Denuvo weren't wrapped around it), Denuvo writes parts of it into DWORDs (32-bit values) scattered around the .vm section, the region of the executable where the VM code lives.2

This is the core idea, and it's a good one. On the wrong machine nothing says "invalid license": you just get a wrong constant, and the game usually crashes somewhere far away from where the problem started. Dunn also describes so-called pre-OEP checks: before the game reaches its original entry point, they decrypt some constants and compare them to expected values; on a mismatch, Denuvo deletes the local license and asks for a new one.2 That's the mechanism behind the "new hardware, new activation" experience from the previous section.

As Dunn points out, it also means a cracker can't just generate a fake token. They either recover every stolen constant and put it back in the binary, or they make every machine look like the one the token was issued for. Remember that second option.

04What goes into the fingerprint

Dunn's write-up lists the sources a recent Denuvo build reads.2 Broadly, they fall into a few groups: what the CPU reports about itself, information requested directly from the Windows kernel, data Windows shares with every process, details that pin down your exact Windows build, and a few ordinary system properties. Notice how many of them deliberately avoid the normal Windows API, which is the easiest layer to hook. The Windows API is the set of documented functions programs normally use to ask the system for information, and hooking means intercepting one of those calls so it returns whatever you want. It's the first thing anyone tampering with a program tries, so a fingerprint built on it would be easy to fake.

Finding all of them is the hard part of any attack. Heumann, who spent his free time on Hogwarts Legacy's Denuvo in 2023–2024, ran the game inside the Qiling emulation framework (a tool that simulates a CPU and an operating system in software, so every instruction the program runs and every answer it receives can be observed and changed), faked one feature at a time and watched whether a valid token got rejected. Finding most features took about two months. Finding the last one took three more, and he only found it by accident.3

05The VM, and why reading it hurts

In Dunn's description, the functions holding stolen constants don't run as normal x86. They're translated into bytecode for a custom virtual CPU, an instruction set Denuvo invents and no real processor understands, and a small interpreter embedded in the game executes it one instruction at a time. For an analyst this means the usual tools stop helping: a disassembler shows you the interpreter, not the program it's running, so first you have to work out what the made-up instruction set means. Code virtualization isn't new (VMProtect and Themida do it too), but Denuvo's VM has some nasty personal touches.2

Each bytecode instruction is carried out by a small piece of native code called a handler, and the interpreter has to jump from one handler to the next. According to his analysis, dispatch goes through a handler table: each handler computes the address of the next one and jumps there by pushing it onto the stack and executing ret. Normally ret means "go back to whoever called me"; used like this it becomes a jump whose destination only exists while the program runs, which makes control flow miserable to follow statically, that is, by reading the code without running it. Bytecode words are stored encrypted and decrypted on the fly. And instead of keeping values in tidy virtual registers, Denuvo can spread a single value across stack slots, bit by bit. Dunn calls that one his favourite feature, which tells you something about reverse engineers.2

On top of that, Dunn found instructions rewritten as Mixed Boolean-Arithmetic (MBA) expressions: formulas that mix +, -, * with &, |, ^ so that something trivial becomes unreadable but stays mathematically identical. A textbook identity: x + y equals (x ^ y) + 2*(x & y). Now chain dozens of those, and a plain mov rax, rbx turns into a page of boolean soup. Dunn used the Z3 theorem prover, a solver that can check whether two formulas give the same result for every possible input, to confirm that the rewritten expressions really are equivalent to the originals.2 Amusingly, Denuvo's own researchers also publish on the other side of the problem: SiMBA, a fast algorithm to simplify linear MBA expressions, came out of Denuvo GmbH in 2022.9 If you build the maze, you'd better know how to solve it.

Two more details from Dunn's write-up that I really like. First, Denuvo needs randomness but doesn't trust RDRAND (the CPU's built-in random number instruction) or OS APIs, which are easy to fake. It uses values already sitting in native registers instead, which are basically guaranteed to change (in a game they can depend on anything, down to your character losing health), and does modular arithmetic on them to pick between nine semantically identical CPUID handlers (CPUID is the instruction a program uses to ask the processor its make, model and features, one of the main fingerprint ingredients). Patch one and the next run may take another. Second, the CPUID instruction itself is kept encrypted: the VM decrypts it, runs it and re-encrypts it, behind a spinlock (a lock that makes other threads wait in a loop until it's released) that is, of course, also encrypted.2 Tracing the decryption of a single constant took Dunn's tooling over ten million x86 instructions.

06The tripwires

Obfuscation slows you down. Integrity checks punish you for touching anything. Denuvo's are designed so that the usual reverse engineering toolbox breaks itself:

Dunn describes CRC checks (checksums: a short number computed from a block of code that changes if a single byte does) that scan critical VM handlers, like the ones that execute CPUID and SYSCALL (the instruction that hands a request to the Windows kernel), for modifications. Constants are also derived from arbitrary byte ranges of the protected section. That matters because the two most common ways of poking at a program both write into its code: a debugger sets a software breakpoint by replacing an instruction with one that stops the program, and an inline hook overwrites the start of a function with a jump to the attacker's code. Either one silently changes the result of some unrelated calculation later.2 You don't get an error. You get a crash ten minutes into the game, in a different function, which is much worse.

Then there's the stack, the area of memory where a program keeps local variables and return addresses, with the stack pointer marking where the part in use ends. Dunn and Heumann both found that Denuvo stores important data above and below the stack pointer, in space that normal code would consider free. When an exception fires, for a hardware breakpoint for example (one set through the CPU's debug registers, precisely so the code doesn't have to be modified), Windows writes an EXCEPTION_RECORD, its description of what just happened, right there and overwrites it.2,3 Heumann put it plainly: this "makes hardware breakpoints and many other techniques either extremely difficult to use, or even impossible". He ended up writing his own small debugger that swallows those exceptions before they reach the game, and using a hypervisor for EPT hooks where patching was too tedious. EPT is the CPU feature a hypervisor uses to control how the system sees memory, so it can redirect execution without changing a single byte the game can read. Total count: around 2,000 hooks and patches, just to get the game to the start screen on a second machine.3

And it isn't a one-time check at launch: according to Heumann, the fingerprint is re-verified while you play.3 Which brings us to the question everybody actually cares about.

07So does it kill performance?

The honest answer is: it depends on where the checks are placed, and that's decided during integration, game by game.

Heumann instrumented Hogwarts Legacy and logged every time execution entered Denuvo. The log fired about once every few seconds, sometimes not at all, and bunched up during loading screens and scene changes. "I don't see any spamming happening, unlike in other DRMs who interact in every frame." He couldn't measure a relevant overhead and concluded that Denuvo "does not deserve all the hate it gets".3

Now the counterexamples. In April 2018, Tekken 7 director Katsuhiro Harada confirmed that frame rate drops when certain moves hit, like Akuma's Shakunetsu Hadouken, came from the "anti-tamper 3rd party middleware", and that changing PC settings wouldn't help.6 A check sitting on a hit effect is a check sitting in a hot path. According to EventHubs, Bandai Namco eventually removed Denuvo from the game in October 2021.10

In 2021, Digital Foundry found that the cracked version of Resident Evil Village didn't stutter where the retail one did. There, Denuvo was layered on top of Capcom's own anti-tamper, and Denuvo said it couldn't comment on Capcom's implementation; Capcom's patch brought the retail game roughly to the cracked version's level.1,7

So both camps are partly right. The protection itself isn't inherently slow. But it hands developers a powerful tool for putting expensive, obfuscated code anywhere they like, and some of them put it in the wrong place.

08Twelve years of cat and mouse

In the early years Denuvo looked close to unbeatable. As Wikipedia and TorrentFreak recount, Chinese group 3DM needed about a month for Dragon Age: Inquisition, and in January 2016 its founder, Bird Sister, wrote after struggling with Just Cause 3 that "in two years time I'm afraid there will be no free games to play in the world".1,5 3DM then stopped cracking games for a year.

The prediction aged badly. According to Wikipedia's timeline, by October 2017, games like Middle-earth: Shadow of War and FIFA 18 were cracked within hours of release, and in December 2018 Hitman 2 was cracked three days before its release.1 After that, according to the same timeline, launch-day cracks disappeared for years: the next one only came in May 2026, when voices38 cracked LEGO Batman: Legacy of the Dark Knight on release. Also in 2026 the approach changed again, with hypervisor-based bypasses that don't touch the game at all: they sit below Windows and make every machine answer the fingerprint questions the way the token expects.1 That's exactly the "second option" from section 03, and it deserves its own post.

Dunn's write-up also sketches how the technical attacks evolved. In the early days, crackers went after the hardware checks one by one. Denuvo answered with countermeasures like the stack trick from section 06, which make that kind of patching break the game. Dunn knew of only one Denuvo binary that had ever been completely restored. And in January 2025 he suggested that a hypervisor could spoof all the hardware information, warning that doing it without side effects would be the hard part.2 About a year later, that's roughly what happened.

Throughout, Denuvo never really sold "uncrackable". It sold time.

09The economics of buying time

How much is that time worth? The best public attempt to answer it is a study by William Volckmann, which looked at 86 Denuvo-protected Steam games released between 2014 and 2022. Since sales figures are mostly private, it used new Steam reviews and player counts as a proxy. A crack in the first week was associated with roughly 20% less revenue than if the protection had held. After about twelve weeks, the effect was close to zero.8

That matches what publishers do in practice. Plenty of games drop Denuvo months or years after launch, like Tekken 7 above,10 once the sales window it protects has closed. Sometimes much sooner: Rage 2 shipped with Denuvo on Steam but without it on Bethesda's own launcher, that version was cracked immediately, and Denuvo was removed from the Steam release two days later.1 Paying to protect a door when the window next to it is open is a hard sell.

10What defenders can take from it

Whatever you think of DRM, Denuvo is a well-engineered piece of client-side protection, and its design choices generalise. Don't write checks, write dependencies. A boolean can be flipped; a constant that only exists once the environment is right can't. Make failure quiet and far away. A crash ten functions later costs an attacker far more than an error dialog. Make the toolbox break itself: if breakpoints and hooks corrupt state, every observation has a price.

But the same design also shows the ceiling of the approach. Everything ultimately hinges on the program's ability to observe its environment honestly: what CPUID says, what the kernel returns, what sits in a shared memory page. On hardware the attacker controls, that's an assumption, not a guarantee. Denuvo made the binary extremely hard to change. It couldn't make the machine tell the truth.

What I respect about Denuvo, as an engineer, is that it never pretended to solve an unsolvable problem. It took a fight it was always going to lose eventually and made it slow, expensive and painful enough to matter commercially. In security, buying time on purpose is a legitimate strategy, as long as you know that's what you're buying.
